Subduction visualizer

September 30, 2026 · Written by Brayden Noh

Here we simulate the subduction of bonded grains and vary their bond stiffness (Young’s modulus) and strength (tensile and shear, set equal) across three different viscosity values, equal to 2 × 1018, 2 × 1019 and 2 × 1020 Pa s, assuming a convergence rate of 10 cm/yr and a 15 km thick plate. The site is designed so that you first select the mantle drag and then pick the combination of stiffness and strength that you wish to see. The time slider records from the initial condition of a coherent slab with a weak angled cut to the final step of the simulation.

The grains are colored by their von Mises strain, computed following Jin et al. (2021). Each Delaunay triangle of the first frame gets its exact deformation gradient F, the Green–Lagrange strain E = (FTF − I)/2 and εvm = √(½ tr E′2) of its deviatoric part.

mantle drag

Columns: log10 bond strength σc. Rows: log10 bond stiffness E. Click a run to open it below; the arrow keys move the selection.

Some remarks are immediate. In the left column (the weakest bonds), the plate fails near the piston, where it piles up and thickens, so even though the weak cut is already in place, the bonds are not strong enough for the plate to reach the cut, let alone slide across it. Across the stiffness and strength space, the upper triangular region of the matrix subducts coherently and the lower one does not, which means that a stiffer plate needs stronger bonds to stay in one piece. One way to think about why is that subduction requires the plate to bend into a certain geometry (to get under the overriding plate), which means it must take on some strain (call it ε). Since stress is modulus times strain (σ = Eε), the same ε in a stiffer plate means a higher stress on its bonds, so we need a higher strength for the plate not to break. In a way, the diagonal line is where these two compete, with coherent subduction on one side and breaking apart on the other (although the line is shallower than one-to-one, since a stiffer plate also bends over a wider hinge and needs less strain). The mantle also makes it harder to subduct. For the plate to go down, it has to push the mantle out of the way, and a more viscous mantle resists this more, which means the piston has to push harder. So the slab stays flat under the overriding plate.
